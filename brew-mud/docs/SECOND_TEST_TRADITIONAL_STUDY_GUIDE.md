## 1. Yeast and other brewing microbes

### What a yeast is

Brewing yeast are **unicellular eukaryotes**: single-celled fungi whose cells have a membrane-bound nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are **prokaryotes**, which lack a membrane-bound nucleus.

### Organisms in brewing

| Organism | Role in brewing | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* | Ale yeast | Generally ferments at warmer temperatures than lager yeast |
| *Saccharomyces pastorianus* | Lager yeast | Cooler fermentation and bottom settling; a hybrid of *S. cerevisiae* and *S. eubayanus* |
| *Brettanomyces* | Wild or specialty yeast | Funky, barnyard, or horse-blanket aromas |
| Lactic acid bacteria (*Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus*) | Souring | Produce the lactic acid that makes sour beer sour |

Lactic acid bacteria differ in what they make:

- **Homofermentative** bacteria produce primarily lactic acid.
- **Heterofermentative** bacteria produce a mixture, such as lactic acid, ethanol, CO₂, and acetic acid.

### From wild fermentation to selected cultures

Spontaneous fermentation is variable because it relies on whatever organisms are present. Isolating single colonies separates the organisms so a brewer can test each one and choose cultures with useful fermentation traits. Isolation alone does not guarantee good performance, and bubbles in a starter do not prove the culture is pure yeast.

## 2. Sugar transport and fermentation

### Getting maltose into the cell

**Maltose** is a disaccharide made of two glucose units, and it is the main fermentable sugar in wort.

1. **Uptake.** Maltose crosses the plasma membrane by **proton-coupled active transport** (symport). Protons flowing down their gradient carry maltose into the cell against its own gradient.
2. **Paying for it.** The cell hydrolyzes **ATP** to keep pumping protons out and maintain that gradient. That is why the uptake counts as active transport even though the protons themselves move downhill.
3. **Splitting.** Inside the cell, **maltase** hydrolyzes maltose into two glucose molecules, which enter glycolysis.

### Glucose repression

When glucose is abundant, it **represses** the machinery yeast use to take up and metabolize maltose. In a high-glucose wort, fermentation can slow down once the glucose runs out, while the yeast switch their maltose genes back on. Adding sugar gradually can reduce both strong glucose repression and osmotic stress.

### Glycolysis

| Feature | Glycolysis |
|---|---|
| Starting material | One glucose (six carbons) |
| Product | Two pyruvate (three carbons each) |
| ATP | Uses 2 ATP and makes 4, for a net gain of **2 ATP** per glucose |
| Electron carrier | Reduces NAD⁺ to **NADH** |
| Oxygen | Not directly required; runs with or without oxygen |

### Alcoholic fermentation

Yeast convert pyruvate to **acetaldehyde** and then to **ethanol**, releasing **CO₂**. These steps make no additional ATP. Their job is to reoxidize NADH back to **NAD⁺**. Without that recycling, NAD⁺ would run out and glycolysis would stall.

### Fermenting even when oxygen is present

The **Crabtree effect**: at high sugar concentrations, yeast favor fermentation even when oxygen is available. Fermentation yields less ATP per glucose than respiration, but rapid sugar use and ethanol production can put competing microbes at a disadvantage.

## 3. Oxygen and flavor

### Why yeast need oxygen early

Brewers oxygenate fresh wort even though they want fermentation. Yeast use that early oxygen to make **sterols** (mainly **ergosterol**) and **unsaturated fatty acids** (oxygen is needed to introduce their double bonds). Both are essential membrane components for growing cells. Because of the Crabtree effect, the high-sugar wort still ferments and produces ethanol while the yeast use oxygen to build membranes.

Oxygen supply also changes how the cell distributes **acetyl-CoA**, which is used both for membrane lipids and for making acetate esters. Oxygen limitation can therefore change ester production as well as yeast growth.

### Flavor compounds

| Compound or class | Typical flavor | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Isoamyl acetate (an ester) | Banana | Alcohol acetyltransferase joins isoamyl alcohol with an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA |
| Higher (fusel) alcohols | Alcohol character; also ingredients for esters | Amino acid synthesis and breakdown |
| 4-vinyl guaiacol (a phenolic) | Clove | Yeast **ferulate decarboxylase** converts barley-derived ferulic acid |
| Chlorophenols | Plastic, medicinal, adhesive bandage | Phenols react with chlorine from water or bleach; noticeable at very low levels |
| Diacetyl | Butter or butterscotch | A fermentation byproduct that yeast can remove during maturation |

Warmer conditions that promote rapid yeast growth tend to increase higher-alcohol production. Nutrients and agitation can also contribute.

A wheat beer's banana and clove aromas come from two different pathways: banana from an ester (isoamyl acetate), clove from a phenolic (4-vinyl guaiacol).

### Removing diacetyl

During maturation, active yeast **take up diacetyl and reduce it** to less flavor-active products. A **diacetyl rest** keeps active yeast in contact with the beer long enough to do this. If yeast settle early or are removed immediately after primary fermentation, the beer can stay buttery.

A second approach prevents diacetyl instead of removing it. The enzyme **ALDC** (alpha-acetolactate decarboxylase) diverts the precursor alpha-acetolactate to acetoin before diacetyl can form.

## 4. Sulfur compounds

| Compound | Effect | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) | Rotten eggs | A sulfur off-aroma |
| Dimethyl sulfide (DMS) | Cooked corn or cooked vegetables | Forms from heated S-methyl methionine (SMM) |
| Bisulfite | Helpful antioxidant | Limits oxidation and masks some staling compounds |

### Controlling DMS

- **SMM** forms in germinating barley. Very pale malts can contain more of it.
- Heating converts SMM into DMS. DMS is volatile, so a **vigorous, uncovered boil** lets it escape with the steam.
- After the boil, a long **hot stand** keeps converting SMM into DMS while nothing removes it. **Rapid cooling** limits that further formation.
- The lower-DMS combination is a vigorous uncovered boil followed by rapid cooling.

DMS and H₂S are different molecules with different aromas and sources. A beer can smell of cooked corn without any rotten-egg aroma.

### Bisulfite and flavor stability

Bisulfite acts as an antioxidant and reacts with carbonyl compounds. One of these is **trans-2-nonenal**, which is associated with wet-cardboard staling. Yeast **sulfite reductase** converts sulfite toward sulfide. Eliminating that enzyme lets more sulfite accumulate, which can improve flavor stability.

## 5. Yeast domestication

- Modern beer yeasts carry the marks of selection by brewers. One study traced a modern beer-yeast lineage to about 1600. That dates the lineage, not the first time humans selected yeast, which could have begun earlier.
- **Farmhouse yeasts** retain stress tolerance that some industrial strains have lost. Domestication outcomes depend on which traits brewers selected.
- Many modern beer yeasts make no clove aroma. The trait can be switched off by DNA changes **without deleting the genes**.

## 6. Browning chemistry

| Process | Required ingredients | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Maillard browning | A reducing sugar **plus an amino group** (from an amino acid or protein), with heat | Needs both reactants |
| Caramelization | **Sugar alone**, with heat | Occurs without amino groups |

Both processes produce brown, colored products, so color alone cannot tell you which one occurred. The starting ingredients distinguish them.

## Answer key and explanations

1. Brewing yeast are unicellular eukaryotes (single-celled fungi). Their cells have a membrane-bound nucleus; prokaryotes such as bacteria and archaea do not.
2. *S. cerevisiae* is the standard ale yeast and generally ferments warmer. *S. pastorianus* is the lager yeast, associated with cooler fermentation and bottom settling. It is a hybrid of *S. cerevisiae* and *S. eubayanus*.
3. *Brettanomyces* can add funky, barnyard, or horse-blanket aromas. Lactic acid bacteria such as *Lactobacillus* and *Pediococcus* produce lactic acid, which makes sour beer sour.
4. Homofermentative bacteria produce primarily lactic acid. Heterofermentative bacteria produce a mixture of products, such as lactic acid, ethanol, CO₂, and acetic acid.
5. Maltose enters by proton-coupled active transport (symport). Inside the cell, maltase hydrolyzes it into two glucose molecules, which enter glycolysis.
6. The downhill flow of protons drives maltose uphill against its own gradient, and the cell spends ATP to maintain the proton gradient. The energy cost makes the overall process active.
7. Abundant glucose represses the machinery for maltose uptake and metabolism. Yeast use the glucose first, and maltose use can lag until the maltose genes are switched back on.
8. One glucose becomes two pyruvate. Glycolysis uses 2 ATP and makes 4, for a net gain of 2 ATP. NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH. Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen.
9. Converting pyruvate through acetaldehyde to ethanol (releasing CO₂) reoxidizes NADH to NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep running. These steps make no additional ATP.
10. At high sugar concentrations, yeast favor fermentation even when oxygen is present.
11. Yeast use early oxygen to make sterols (mainly ergosterol) and unsaturated fatty acids, which they need to build membranes for new cells.
12. Isoamyl acetate: banana. 4-vinyl guaiacol: clove. Diacetyl: butter or butterscotch. H₂S: rotten eggs. DMS: cooked corn or cooked vegetables. Chlorophenols: plastic, medicinal, or adhesive bandage.
13. Higher alcohols come from amino acid synthesis and breakdown. Warmer conditions that promote rapid yeast growth increase them, and nutrients and agitation can also contribute.
14. DMS forms when heat converts S-methyl methionine (SMM), which forms in germinating barley. A vigorous uncovered boil lets DMS escape, and rapid cooling afterward limits further formation.
15. Bisulfite acts as an antioxidant and reacts with carbonyl compounds such as trans-2-nonenal (wet cardboard), masking some staling flavors.
16. Maillard browning needs a reducing sugar plus an amino group from an amino acid or protein, with heat. Caramelization needs only sugar and heat. Both produce brown color.
17. Glucose repressed the maltose uptake and metabolism machinery. When the glucose ran out, the yeast had to switch maltose genes back on, causing a lag. Adding sugar gradually reduces strong glucose repression and osmotic stress.
18. Because of the Crabtree effect, high sugar makes yeast ferment even with oxygen present. The oxygen is used mainly to build sterols and unsaturated fatty acids for new membranes.
19. Too little active yeast remained to take up and reduce diacetyl during maturation. A diacetyl rest with active yeast would let it be removed. Alternatively, ALDC diverts alpha-acetolactate to acetoin so diacetyl never forms.
20. Pale malt can be high in SMM. The covered boil kept DMS from escaping, and the long hot stand kept converting SMM into DMS with nothing removing it. Use a vigorous uncovered boil and cool the wort rapidly.
21. No. DMS (cooked corn) and H₂S (rotten eggs) are different molecules with different aromas and sources. DMS from heated SMM can be present without any H₂S.
22. Banana comes from an ester, isoamyl acetate, made from isoamyl alcohol and acetyl-CoA. Clove comes from a phenolic, 4-vinyl guaiacol, made when yeast ferulate decarboxylase converts ferulic acid.
23. Sulfite reductase converts sulfite toward sulfide. Without it, more sulfite accumulates. Sulfite (as bisulfite) acts as an antioxidant and reacts with staling carbonyls such as trans-2-nonenal, so the beer can stay fresh longer.
24. Domestication outcomes depend on which traits brewers selected. Different brewing environments and selection histories can preserve different traits, such as stress tolerance in farmhouse yeasts.
25. The date describes when that one lineage arose. Selection of yeast could have begun much earlier, so the study cannot show when humans first used yeast.
